# Anti-AI PM Writing Style — A Field Guide

> **What this is:** A reference for PMs who use AI in their workflow and want their outputs to read like they came from a person who's done the thinking — not a tool that's done the generating. Fifteen patterns to eliminate, with concrete before/after examples and five litmus tests to run before finalising any document.

---

## Why This Matters

Product managers live and die by credibility. When your PRD, strategy doc, or stakeholder email reads like it was generated by an AI, you lose trust — not because the content is wrong, but because it signals you haven't done the thinking. Executives, engineers, and designers have increasingly sharp instincts for AI-generated text. They might not call it out directly, but they'll skim faster, push back harder, and trust less.

The patterns below are the most common tells in AI-generated PM writing. Avoiding them isn't about hiding AI usage — it's about producing writing that reflects genuine thought, specific context, and real judgment.

---

## Content Patterns to Eliminate

### 1. The Significance Inflation Pattern

**The tell:** AI text constantly inflates the importance of whatever it's describing. Every feature is "pivotal," every metric is "crucial," every decision "marks a significant shift."

**In PM writing, this looks like:**
> "This initiative represents a pivotal shift in our approach to authentication, underscoring our commitment to security excellence while enhancing the user experience landscape."

**Why it fails:** Real PMs are specific about impact magnitude. They say "this reduces abandonment by 15%" — not "this represents a significant improvement." Inflation breeds skepticism.

**The fix:** Replace significance claims with evidence. Let the reader judge importance from the facts.

> "This change reduced document capture failures from 3.2 attempts to 1.4 on mobile — approximately 2,100 fewer abandoned verifications per week."

---

### 2. The Hollow Trade-Off Pattern

**The tell:** AI acknowledges trade-offs exist but immediately resolves them with vague language, making it seem like there are no real costs. The structure is: "While [concern], [reassurance]."

**In PM writing, this looks like:**
> "While there may be some increase in engineering complexity, the long-term benefits of this approach far outweigh the short-term costs, ensuring a more robust and scalable solution."

**Why it fails:** This says nothing. What complexity? How much? What long-term benefits specifically? "Far outweigh" is a claim, not an argument.

**The fix:** Name the trade-off with specifics. State what you're giving up and why it's worth it.

> "This approach adds approximately 3 weeks to the timeline and requires the mobile team to defer the push notification work. We're accepting that cost because the document capture fix addresses 3x more abandoned users than push notifications would recover."

---

### 3. The Comprehensive Emptiness Pattern

**The tell:** AI generates text that covers every conceivable angle but says nothing specific about any of them. It reads like a table of contents masquerading as analysis.

**In PM writing, this looks like:**
> "Our identity verification strategy encompasses user experience optimisation, security enhancement, regulatory compliance, scalability considerations, and cross-platform consistency, all while maintaining our commitment to innovation and customer-centricity."

**Why it fails:** This sentence could describe literally any identity product at any company. It contains zero information. Every word is technically correct and collectively meaningless.

**The fix:** Pick the one or two things that actually matter and go deep. Omit the rest or address them separately.

> "Our immediate priority is mobile document capture — it's the single largest funnel leak and the one we have the highest confidence we can fix in six weeks."

---

### 4. The False Expertise Pattern

**The tell:** AI peppers text with domain terminology to appear knowledgeable without demonstrating actual understanding. Terms are used correctly but superficially — like someone who memorised a glossary.

**In PM writing, this looks like:**
> "Leveraging our RBAC framework in conjunction with ABAC policies, we can implement a zero-trust architecture that enables fine-grained authorisation controls, ensuring compliance with PSD2 SCA requirements while maintaining a frictionless user experience."

**Why it fails:** Every term is technically correct, but they're assembled like a keyword checklist rather than a coherent argument. A real PM would explain WHY this specific combination matters for their specific use case.

**The fix:** Use technical terms when precision requires it, but connect them to concrete product decisions.

> "We need ABAC (not just RBAC) for the consent layer because Open Banking requires transaction-level permissions — a user might approve read-only access to their balance but not to their transaction history. RBAC can't express that granularity."

## Language Patterns to Eliminate

### 5. The AI Vocabulary Footprint

**The tell:** Certain words appear with dramatically higher frequency in AI text than in human writing. Their presence — especially in clusters — is the strongest signal of AI generation.

**Words to eliminate from PM writing:**

| AI Crutch | Human Alternative |
|---|---|
| "delve into" | "examine," "look at," "dig into" |
| "crucial" / "pivotal" / "vital" | State the specific importance, or omit |
| "landscape" (figurative) | "market," "space," "environment" — or just name the thing |
| "foster" / "cultivate" | "build," "create," "encourage" |
| "underscore" / "highlight" (as verbs) | "show," "reveal," "confirm" |
| "robust" | "strong," "reliable," "durable" — or say what makes it robust |
| "seamless" | Describe what the user actually experiences |
| "holistic" | "complete," "end-to-end" — or just describe what's included |
| "leverage" (as a verb) | "use" |
| "tapestry" / "mosaic" (figurative) | Don't. Describe the thing. |
| "garner" | "get," "earn," "attract" |
| "interplay" | "relationship," "tension," "connection" |
| "Moreover" / "Furthermore" / "Additionally" (as sentence openers) | Use natural transitions or restructure |
| "It's important to note that" | Just state the thing |
| "In conclusion" | Conclusions should be obvious from context |
| "This underscores the importance of" | State why it matters directly |

**The cluster test:** If your paragraph contains three or more words from this table, rewrite it. One is coincidental. Three is a pattern.

---

### 6. The Rule of Three Addiction

**The tell:** AI defaults to triple-adjective lists and three-item enumerations constantly. "Fast, reliable, and secure." "Scalable, maintainable, and extensible." "Users, stakeholders, and partners."

**In PM writing, this looks like:**
> "Our platform delivers speed, security, and simplicity — creating a verification experience that is intuitive, efficient, and trustworthy for users across all devices, channels, and touchpoints."

**Why it fails:** Real writing varies its rhythm. Sometimes one adjective is enough. Sometimes four items are the truth. The relentless tripling signals algorithmic composition.

**The fix:** Use the number of items that actually exists. If two things matter, say two. If five things are relevant, say five. If one word captures it, use one word.

---

### 7. The Copula Avoidance Pattern

**The tell:** AI replaces simple "is/are/has" constructions with fancier alternatives: "serves as," "stands as," "represents," "boasts," "features."

**Examples:**

| AI version | Human version |
|---|---|
| "The dashboard serves as a centralised hub for authentication metrics." | "The dashboard shows authentication metrics." |
| "Our IDV module stands as the most critical component in the onboarding flow." | "IDV is the most critical step in onboarding." |
| "The platform features real-time risk scoring capabilities." | "The platform has real-time risk scoring." |

**The fix:** Use "is," "are," "has," and "shows" freely. They're not boring — they're clear.

---

### 8. The Elegant Variation Pattern

**The tell:** AI avoids repeating words by cycling through synonyms — "users" becomes "customers" becomes "individuals" becomes "end-users" — sometimes within a single paragraph, referring to the same people.

**In PM writing, this looks like:**
> "Users enter their details in the first step. Customers then verify their identity through document capture. Individuals who complete this process receive account access. End-users can then begin transacting."

**Why it fails:** In PM writing, precision matters. "Users," "customers," "individuals," and "end-users" are not synonyms — they imply different segments, relationships, and stages. Inconsistent terminology creates confusion in specs that engineers need to build from.

**The fix:** Pick one term and use it consistently. Repetition in PM writing is a feature, not a bug — it creates precision.

---

### 9. The Negative Parallelism Overuse

**The tell:** AI overuses "not just... but also" and "not only... but" constructions to appear balanced and thoughtful.

**In PM writing:**
> "This is not just a technical improvement — it's a fundamental shift in how we approach user trust. It's not about speed, it's about confidence."

**The fix:** State what it IS. If the contrast is genuinely important, use it once. If every other sentence has a "not X, but Y" structure, you're writing AI prose.

## Structural Patterns to Eliminate

### 10. The Challenges-and-Future-Outlook Ending

**The tell:** AI-generated documents frequently end with a section acknowledging "challenges" followed by optimistic speculation about the future. The structure is rigid: "Despite [positive qualities], [subject] faces challenges including [list]. However, [optimistic future statement]."

**In PM writing, this looks like:**
> "Despite significant progress in our authentication capabilities, several challenges remain, including legacy system integration, cross-platform consistency, and evolving regulatory requirements. However, with continued investment in innovation and our strong team, we are well-positioned to address these challenges and deliver a world-class identity platform."

**Why it fails:** This is boilerplate. It could end any document about any product. It adds zero information and signals that the writer ran out of genuine things to say.

**The fix:** End with a specific next step, a concrete decision needed, or nothing at all. If you've made your point, stop.

---

### 11. The Exhaustive Symmetry Pattern

**The tell:** AI generates perfectly balanced sections — every pro has a con, every strength has a weakness, every section is the same length. Real analysis is asymmetric because reality is asymmetric.

**In PM writing, this looks like:** Three options evaluated with exactly four pros and four cons each, each bullet exactly one sentence, all sections identical length.

**Why it fails:** This symmetry signals algorithmic generation. In reality, Option A might have one fatal flaw that makes the other seven points irrelevant. Option B might have three interconnected strengths that compound.

**The fix:** Let the analysis be shaped by the facts. If one option has a single fatal flaw, say so in one sentence and move on. If another needs detailed nuance, give it the space it needs.

---

### 12. The Universal Opening Pattern

**The tell:** AI opens PM documents with broad contextual statements that provide no new information to the intended audience.

**In PM writing, this looks like:**
> "In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape, identity verification has become increasingly important for financial institutions. As regulatory requirements grow more complex and user expectations continue to rise, organisations must find innovative approaches to balance security with user experience."

**Why it fails:** Your VP of Product already knows this. Your engineering team already knows this. This opening exists because the AI doesn't know your specific situation, so it fills space with generic truth.

**The fix:** Open with the specific thing that matters right now. The first sentence should contain information the reader doesn't already have.

> "IDV mobile abandonment hit 46% last week — the highest since the new flow launched in January. Here's what's happening and what I recommend."

## Formatting Patterns to Eliminate

### 13. The Boldface Everything Pattern

**The tell:** AI bolds every concept, framework name, and supposedly important phrase, creating text that looks like a textbook index.

**The fix:** Bold sparingly. In PM documents, bold should be used for scanability — section headers, key metric values, recommended actions. Not for every proper noun in the body text.

---

### 14. The Emoji Section Header Pattern

**The tell:** Headers decorated with emoji: "🔒 Security Considerations", "📊 Key Metrics", "✅ Next Steps"

**The fix:** Never. Not in PM documents.

---

### 15. The Perfect Paragraph Pattern

**The tell:** Every paragraph is exactly 3–4 sentences. Every section has exactly 2–3 paragraphs. The regularity is the tell.

**The fix:** Vary paragraph length. Some points need one sentence. Some need six. Let content dictate structure, not a template.

---

## The Litmus Tests

Before finalising any PM document, run these:

**The Specificity Test:** Could this sentence have been written about a different product at a different company? If yes, rewrite it with specifics — your product, your data, your users.

**The "So What" Test:** After every paragraph, ask "So what?" If you can't answer with a specific implication or action, the paragraph isn't earning its place.

**The Cluster Test:** Count AI vocabulary words. More than two per page means a rewrite.

**The Colleague Test:** If you emailed this to a peer PM, would they think you wrote it or that an AI wrote it? If there's any doubt, identify the patterns above and fix them.

**The Decision Test:** Does this document make a decision easier, faster, or better? If not, what's it for?

---

## The Five Commandments

1. **Be specific where AI is generic.** Names, numbers, dates, quotes, observed behaviours — specificity is the antidote to AI blandness.

2. **Be asymmetric where AI is balanced.** Real analysis has sharp edges. Some things matter more than others. Let your writing reflect that.

3. **Be direct where AI hedges.** "I recommend X because Y" beats "It could be argued that X might be a viable approach given certain considerations."

4. **Be silent where AI fills space.** If you've made the point, stop. AI adds paragraphs because it optimises for word count. Optimise for impact per word.

5. **Be human where AI is mechanical.** Vary your sentence length. Use "is" and "has" without shame. Repeat terms for precision. Let paragraphs be the length they need to be. Write like someone who has done the work and has something specific to say.

---

*This guide works as a final-pass filter on any PM document. The goal isn't to hide AI usage — it's to ensure every output reflects the specificity, judgment, and directness that PM work demands.*
